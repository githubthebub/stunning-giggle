Interactive · the forgetting curve

Why you forget — and how spacing beats it

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus measured his own memory and found it decays exponentially: steep at first, then leveling off. But every time you successfully recall something, the curve gets flatter — you forget slower. Drag the controls and watch a memory fight the curve.

Review sooner (before you've forgotten much) and the line stays higher — at the cost of more reviews.

Harder retrieval — recalling when you almost forgot — strengthens memory more, so intervals can grow faster and you need fewer reviews. "Desirable difficulty."

The Learning Operating System

Five techniques that actually move the needle

Decades of cognitive-science research keep pointing at the same short list. Most of what students do — highlighting, re-reading, cramming — barely helps. These do. Each card is a protocol you can run this week.

01

Active Recall

Don't re-read — retrieve. Close the book and force your brain to produce the answer. The struggle to remember is what builds the memory, not the looking.

Run it: After any chapter, shut it and write everything you remember on a blank page. Only then check what you missed. That gap is your study list.
Roediger & Karpickethe testing effect
02

Spaced Repetition

Review just as you're about to forget. Spacing the same total study time across days beats one long session — sometimes doubling long-term recall for zero extra effort.

Run it: Review at expanding gaps — 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month. The flashcard engine below schedules this for you automatically.
Ebbinghaus · Cepeda et al.the spacing effect
03

Interleaving

Mix problem types instead of blocking them. It feels harder and slower — and that's the point. Interleaving trains you to choose the right approach, not just execute a known one.

Run it: Instead of 20 problems of type A then 20 of B, shuffle them. You'll score worse in practice and far better on the test.
Rohrer & Taylordiscrimination
04

Elaboration & the Feynman Technique

Explain it simply, out loud, as if teaching a 12-year-old. The moment you stumble, you've found the hole in your understanding — jargon can no longer hide it.

Run it: Write the concept in plain words. Hit a gap? Go back to the source, fill it, simplify again. Repeat until it flows.
Feynman · self-explanationgeneration effect
05

Deliberate Practice

Expertise isn't hours logged — it's hours spent at the edge of your ability with immediate feedback. Practice the specific thing you can't do yet, not the thing you've already mastered.

Run it: Isolate the weakest sub-skill, drill it with a tight feedback loop, and raise the difficulty the instant it gets comfortable.
K. Anders Ericssonthe edge of ability
＋

Two things that don't work (much)

Highlighting & re-reading feel productive because they feel fluent — but fluency is not memory. Cramming can pass tomorrow's test, then evaporates by next week.

The rule of thumb: if a study method feels easy, it's probably not building durable memory. Difficulty is the signal.
Dunlosky et al., 2013

01

Win the first 3 seconds

Every viewer decides almost instantly whether to stay. Open on the most interesting moment — the payoff, the stakes, the promise — never the throat-clearing intro.

Apply it: Whatever your best moment is, some version of it belongs in second one. Then earn the right to slow down.
02

The title & thumbnail are the product

Most creators treat packaging as an afterthought. The best treat the idea, title, and thumbnail as the actual product — and build the content to deliver on that promise. A great video no one clicks does not exist.

Apply it: Write the headline first. If you can't make the promise irresistible and honest, the idea isn't ready.
03

Open loops & curiosity gaps

The brain hates an unresolved question. Pose one early — "by the end, one of these will be gone" — and the audience stays to close the loop. Then open the next one before you close it.

Apply it: Tease the outcome, delay the reveal, and stack a new question on top of each answer.
04

Escalate — never plateau

Attention leaks continuously, so the stakes must keep rising. Each act should feel bigger than the last: more at risk, more surprising, more consequential. Comfort is where audiences leave.

Apply it: Map your piece as a staircase, not a flat hallway. Every landing raises the stakes.
05

Ruthless pacing

Cut anything that doesn't advance the story or the payoff. The most-watched creators re-edit around the retention graph — wherever the line dips, something gets tightened or removed.

Apply it: If a sentence, scene, or slide doesn't earn its seconds, delete it. Density is respect for attention.
◆

The honest version of virality

These are tools for attention — pointed at something worth people's time, they teach and inspire; pointed at nothing, they're just noise. Use them to make true, useful things travel further.

The test: Would a viewer be glad they stayed? If yes, engineer the retention. If no, fix the substance first.

Thinking tools · decide better

Mental models for a sharper mind

Knowledge is inert until you can use it under uncertainty. These are the reusable thinking tools that show up again and again across great decision-makers.

🔁

Inversion

Instead of "how do I succeed?", ask "how would I guarantee failure?" — then avoid those things. Solving the inverse is often easier and catches blind spots forward-thinking misses.

✂️

First-Principles Thinking

Strip an idea down to what you know is true, then reason up from there — instead of arguing by analogy or "how it's always been done." This is where non-obvious answers live.

📉

Opportunity Cost

Every yes is a no to everything else you could do with that time or money. The real cost of a choice is the best alternative you gave up — not the price on the tag.

🎯

Second-Order Thinking

"And then what?" Most people stop at the immediate result. Great decisions come from tracing the consequences of the consequences — two and three moves ahead.

⚖️

Expected Value

Weigh each outcome by its probability, not just its size. A small chance of a huge win — or loss — dominates. Play the odds repeatedly and the math wins.

🪒

Occam's Razor & the Map ≠ Territory

Prefer the simplest explanation that fits the facts — and remember every model is a simplified map. When the map and reality disagree, reality wins. Update the map.
